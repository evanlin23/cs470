\begin{abstract}
When university students seek research mentors or faculty seek cross-disciplinary collaborators, they overwhelmingly search through familiar, densely clustered social circles. However, foundational network science demonstrates that novel, innovative ideas are disproportionately generated across structural holes separating disconnected communities (Burt 1992, 2004) and diffuse through weak ties (Granovetter 1973). Existing academic discovery systems (e.g., Illinois Experts, Google Scholar) match purely on lexical keywords and treat scholars as isolated documents, ignoring graph topology. Conversely, commercial link recommendation heuristics optimize for triadic closure, reinforcing insular echo chambers. To overcome these limitations, we present \textbf{BridgeFinder}, a topology-aware recommendation engine and interactive visualization system designed to discover high-value collaborative ties across structural holes at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (UIUC). In this mid-semester report, we detail our completed system pipeline, resolve topological distance trade-offs between warm boundary bridges (2 hops), deep structural weak ties (3+ hops), and institutional component bridges ($\infty$ hops), and present a mathematically rigorous multi-objective brokerage scoring function. We have preprocessed the complete UIUC co-authorship corpus from OpenAlex (110,482 papers from 2015 to 2024 mapped to 2,574 faculty from the February 2024 UIUC Gray Book) and partitioned it into 18 communities via Louvain modularity optimization ($Q = 0.684$). Finally, we outline a comprehensive, multi-phase assessment methodology comprising: (1) an offline temporal backtest predicting new 2020--2024 cross-community collaborations from 2015--2019 topology against four baselines (Triadic Closure, Topic-Only Cosine, Betweenness Centrality, and Preferential Attachment); (2) a randomized double-blind user evaluation with 15 student participants; and (3) a cognitive walkthrough measuring usability and introduction path clarity.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Structural holes, brokerage, weak ties, community discovery, co-authorship networks, decentralized search, interdisciplinary collaboration.}

\section{Introduction and Objectives}

Modern research universities are vast, decentralized intellectual ecosystems. The February 2024 UIUC Academic Personnel Register (the ``Gray Book'') lists 2,574 tenure-track and specialized faculty holding professor titles across 110 departments~\cite{graybook2024}. While this breadth fosters deep specialization, it creates severe structural fragmentation: separate research groups in computer science, crop sciences, materials science, and biomedical informatics frequently tackle shared computational challenges for years without co-authoring a paper or citing one another's literature.

When students attempt to secure research positions, or when faculty seek collaborators outside their home department, they encounter the \emph{local search dilemma}. Traditional search relies on strong ties and immediate departmental proximity: students approach instructors of their current courses, while faculty consult departmental colleagues. Sociologist Mark Granovetter established that strong ties circulate redundant information because close associates share overlapping networks~\cite{granovetter1973}. In contrast, novel information and innovative opportunities diffuse through \emph{weak ties} acting as local bridges. Ronald Burt extended this into the theory of \emph{structural holes}~\cite{burt1992,burt2004}, showing that individuals spanning gaps between disconnected groups gain substantial competitive advantages: they enjoy early access to diverse knowledge streams, synthesize ideas across domain boundaries, and produce work with higher long-term citation impact.

Despite the proven value of brokerage, existing institutional software fails to support it. The university's official portal, \emph{Illinois Experts}~\cite{experts} (Elsevier Pure), and commercial engines like \emph{Google Scholar} index researchers as independent textual documents. A query for ``graph neural networks for drug discovery'' returns scholars matching the search terms but is blind to graph topology: a colleague who shares five mutual co-authors with the user is ranked identically to an isolated scholar across campus. Conversely, platforms like LinkedIn (``People You May Know'') rely on link prediction heuristics rooted in \emph{triadic closure}~\cite{libennowell2007,gupta2013}. Triadic closure maximizes the probability of edge formation, which systematically reinforces insular echo chambers and penalizes the very structural holes where transformative interdisciplinary research emerges.

\textbf{BridgeFinder} addresses this gap by building a topology-aware recommendation engine and interactive visual map. By modeling the university as a weighted co-authorship network paired with high-dimensional topic embeddings from OpenAlex~\cite{openalex}, BridgeFinder identifies structural holes across research communities and introduces users to complementary researchers on the other side. 

Our core research objectives and falsifiable hypotheses are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item \textbf{Theoretical Formulation:} Formulate a mathematically sound multi-objective brokerage scoring model balancing topic alignment, Burt's constraint reduction, boundary spanning, and path transmission probability while penalizing insular triadic closure.
    \item \textbf{Topological Disambiguation:} Resolve the distance trade-off between warm boundary bridges (2 hops), deep weak ties (3+ hops), and disconnected institutional bridges ($\infty$ hops) to provide actionable introduction chains.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Backtesting ($H_{\text{pred}}$):} Validate that historical graph topology from 2015--2019 predicts actual cross-community collaborations formed in 2020--2024 with significantly higher precision and recall than triadic closure or keyword-only baselines.
    \item \textbf{Impact Verification ($H_{\text{impact}}$):} Test Burt's hypothesis that new cross-community publications bridging structural holes achieve higher citation impact than within-community publications in the same subfields.
    \item \textbf{Human-Centered Validation ($H_{\text{user}}$):} Demonstrate via a randomized double-blind user study that students evaluate BridgeFinder recommendations as substantially more serendipitous (novel) than triadic closure while maintaining high relevance and contact willingness.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Academic Search and Profiling Engines}
Academic discovery on university campuses has historically relied on profile aggregators like \emph{Illinois Experts}~\cite{experts}, VIVO~\cite{vivo2012}, and Google Scholar. Illinois Experts ingests Scopus citation records and indexes faculty based on publication titles, abstracts, and appointments. While effective for known-item lookups (e.g., verifying grant history), these tools treat scholars as independent text corpora with zero representation of relational topology. Consequently, they cannot differentiate between a scholar in the user's immediate laboratory and an equally relevant scholar working in an isolated facility across campus. Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex~\cite{openalex} incorporate vector embeddings for literature discovery, but their recommendation APIs focus on paper co-citation rather than inter-personal social brokerage.

\subsection{Empirical Network Studies of Academia}
Empirical literature in computational social science documents that university collaboration networks are heavily stratified by organizational boundaries. Dahlander and McFarland~\cite{dahlander2013} tracked 15 years of faculty collaboration across Stanford University, showing that absent shared affiliations or physical proximity, collaboration probability decays rapidly over time. Recently, Barley, Choudhury, and Franzoni~\cite{barley2025} conducted a multi-decade natural experiment at UIUC, analyzing how faculty who joined interdisciplinary institutes (such as the Beckman Institute and the Institute for Genomic Biology) significantly diversified their co-authorship networks relative to matched controls. While these studies demonstrate that organizational interventions can bridge structural gaps, they operate post-hoc as sociological analyses; neither developed computational algorithms to proactively recommend high-value structural holes to prospective collaborators.

\subsection{Link Prediction versus Brokerage}
In computer science, automated collaboration recommendation is typically formulated as link prediction. In their seminal work, Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg~\cite{libennowell2007} evaluated topological heuristics (Common Neighbors, Jaccard coefficient, Adamic-Adar, and Preferential Attachment) across co-authorship graphs, finding that heuristics based on triadic closure achieved the highest predictive accuracy for future edge formation. 

However, link prediction optimizes for \emph{likelihood of formation}, which is the theoretical antithesis of \emph{brokerage value}. Triadic closure predicts edges between researchers who already share dense mutual connections (high clustering coefficients). Sociometrically, these represent redundant ties offering little novel information~\cite{burt2004}. Tang et al.~\cite{tang2012} proposed a cross-domain collaboration framework using topic-level random walks, but their model relies on predefined subject categories and does not explicitly evaluate community topological boundaries or Burt's structural hole constraint. BridgeFinder bridges this gap by explicitly penalizing intra-community triadic closure while rewarding inter-community boundary spanning.

\section{Course Concepts \& Network Analysis}

BridgeFinder directly operationalizes core theoretical foundations from CS 470, integrating structural holes and weak ties (Lesson~1), community discovery and modularity (Lesson~10), and small-world decentralized search (Lesson~13).

\subsection{Network Formulation and Graph Representation}
Let $\mathcal{G} = (V, E, W, \mathcal{T})$ denote the UIUC academic collaboration network:
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength{\itemsep}{0.2pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
    \item $V = \{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is the set of $n$ faculty researchers.
    \item $E \subseteq V \times V$ is the set of undirected co-authorship ties.
    \item $W: E \to \mathbb{R}^+$ assigns edge weights based on collaboration volume using Newman's fractional author weighting~\cite{newman2004} to prevent hyper-authored consortium publications from distorting tie strength:
    $w_{ij} = \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{ij}} \frac{1}{|A_p| - 1}$, where $\mathcal{P}_{ij}$ is the set of joint papers, and $|A_p|$ is the author count of paper $p$.
    \item $\mathcal{T} = \{\vec{\tau}_1, \dots, \vec{\tau}_n\}$ associates each researcher $v_i$ with an $L_2$-normalized semantic topic embedding vector $\vec{\tau}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ derived from the OpenAlex topic hierarchy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Community Discovery and Modularity Optimization}
To identify structural holes, the network must first be partitioned into coherent research communities. In Lesson~10, we examined community discovery algorithms. In our proposal pilot, the Girvan-Newman edge-betweenness algorithm~\cite{girvan2002} required 148 seconds on a 150-node subgraph ($O(m^2 n)$), rendering it intractable for a campus graph with thousands of nodes.

Consequently, BridgeFinder utilizes the Louvain algorithm~\cite{blondel2008}, a greedy heuristic optimizing Newman-Girvan modularity $Q$ in $O(n \log n)$ time:
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \left[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right] \delta(c_i, c_j)
\end{equation}
where $A_{ij}$ is the adjacency matrix, $k_i = \sum_j A_{ij}$, $m = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i k_i$, and $\delta(c_i, c_j) = 1$ if nodes $i$ and $j$ share a community. Partitioning our 2015--2019 baseline graph with Louvain yielded 18 communities with high modularity ($Q = 0.684$) in 1.42 seconds. We denote by $C(u)$ the community assignment of node $u$.

\subsection{Resolving Topological Distance: 2 Hops, 3+ Hops, and Disconnected Components}
\label{sec:distance_clarification}

In our proposal feedback, the evaluators noted an apparent inconsistency: \emph{``The text ranks researchers at least three steps away, but Figure 1 shows researchers at 2 hops and with no path.''} 

We clarify and formally resolve this distinction. In network brokerage, topological distance must be evaluated in conjunction with \emph{community boundary membership}. A candidate collaborator $v$ occupies one of three distinct topological tiers relative to seed node $u$:

\subsubsection{Tier 1: Warm Boundary Bridges ($d(u, v) = 2$, $C(u) \neq C(v)$)}
When $d(u, v) = 2$, $u$ and $v$ share a mutual collaborator $w \in N(u) \cap N(v)$, but $(u, v) \notin E$. If $C(u) = C(v)$, this is standard within-community triadic closure. However, if $C(u) \neq C(v)$, the intermediary $w$ is a \emph{boundary spanner} whose edges cross the community interface. The open triad $u \text{---} w \text{---} v$ spans a structural hole between communities $C(u)$ and $C(v)$. 

We classify this as a \textbf{Warm Bridge}. While triadic closure predicts open triads close rapidly, empirical academic networks exhibit persistent structural holes across departmental seams. For a student or junior researcher, Tier~1 candidates provide the highest \emph{actionability}: the shared collaborator $w$ can provide a direct personal introduction, substantially lowering the psychological barrier to outreach.

\subsubsection{Tier 2: Deep Structural Weak Ties ($d(u, v) \ge 3$, $C(u) \neq C(v)$)}
When $d(u, v) \ge 3$, $u$ and $v$ share zero mutual co-authors ($N(u) \cap N(v) = \emptyset$). Any connecting path requires at least two intermediaries ($u \to w_1 \to w_2 \to v$). 

We classify this as a \textbf{Cold / Structural Bridge}. Sociologically, Tier~2 candidates offer maximal non-redundant knowledge arbitrage (Burt 2004): their immediate research environments share almost no overlapping information with $u$'s cluster. However, the introduction path is more distributed, requiring decentralized navigation through weak ties (Granovetter 1973; Kleinberg 2000).

\subsubsection{Tier 3: Component-Spanning Institutional Bridges ($d(u, v) = \infty$)}
Real university networks contain disconnected components. When candidate $v$ resides in a separate connected component, no pure co-authorship path exists ($d(u, v) = \infty$). Rather than discarding these nodes, BridgeFinder introduces \textbf{Institutional Bridges}. We query secondary bipartite networks: shared departmental affiliations, cross-listed courses, and joint institute appointments (e.g., Beckman, NCSA, IGB). An introduction path is synthesized via these institutional intermediaries: $u \to [\text{Home Dept}] \to [\text{Institute}] \to v$.

\calloutbox{Synthesis of Topological Resolution}{BridgeFinder implements a tiered hierarchy: Tier~1 ($d=2$ across community boundaries) provides warm introductions; Tier~2 ($d \ge 3$) surfaces radical, serendipitous brokerage across structural chasms; and Tier~3 ($d=\infty$) synthesizes institutional bridge pathways.}

\subsection{Formal Mathematical Definition of the Brokerage Score}
\label{sec:brokerage_score}

In response to proposal feedback requiring a formal definition of how the brokerage score is computed, we define our multi-objective scoring formulation. Given query seed node $u \in V$ and candidate researcher $v \in V \setminus (N(u) \cup \{u\})$, BridgeFinder computes the composite ranking function:
\begin{align}
    \Phi(u, v) = \; &\alpha \cdot S_{\text{topic}}(u, v) + \beta \cdot H(v) + \gamma \cdot \text{BSI}(v, C(u)) \nonumber \\
    &+ \lambda \cdot P(u, v) - \mu \cdot \text{TCP}(u, v)
    \label{eq:unified_score}
\end{align}
where $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \lambda, \mu \ge 0$ satisfy $\alpha + \beta + \gamma + \lambda = 1$. Each component is defined rigorously:

\subsubsection{1. Semantic Topic Similarity $S_{\text{topic}}(u, v)$}
We evaluate cognitive complementarity using cosine similarity between normalized OpenAlex topic embedding vectors: $S_{\text{topic}}(u, v) = \frac{\vec{\tau}_u \cdot \vec{\tau}_v}{\|\vec{\tau}_u\|_2 \|\vec{\tau}_v\|_2} \in [0, 1]$.

\subsubsection{2. Burt's Node Brokerage Autonomy $H(v)$}
Ronald Burt's network constraint $C_i$ measures the extent to which a node's network is concentrated in a single cohesive clique~\cite{burt1992}. The proportional investment of node $i$ in neighbor $j$ is $p_{ij} = \frac{w_{ij}}{\sum_{k \in N(i)} w_{ik}}$. The dyadic constraint that neighbor $j$ exerts on node $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
    c_{ij} = \left( p_{ij} + \sum_{q \in N(i) \setminus \{j\}} p_{iq} p_{qj} \right)^2
\end{equation}
The aggregate network constraint of candidate $v$ is $C_v = \sum_{j \in N(v)} c_{vj} \in [0, 1]$. A high value indicates a redundant clique; a low value indicates that $v$ spans structural holes. We define node brokerage autonomy as $H(v) = 1 - C_v \in [0, 1]$.

\subsubsection{3. Boundary Spanning Index $\text{BSI}(v, C(u))$}
We measure how effectively $v$ bridges specifically toward user community $C(u)$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{BSI}(v, C(u)) = \theta \cdot \frac{\sum_{w \in N(v) \cap C(u)} w_{vw}}{\sum_{z \in N(v)} w_{vz}} + (1 - \theta) \cdot \frac{|\{C(k) : k \in N(v)\}|}{|C_{\text{all}}|}
\end{equation}
where $\theta \in [0, 1]$ balances collaborative energy directed specifically into $u$'s community versus global community entropy.

\subsubsection{4. Introduction Path Transmission Probability $P(u, v)$}
Drawing from decentralized search (Lesson~13) and tie decay (Lesson~1), edge transmission reliability decays with tie staleness: $\rho(x_m, x_{m+1}) = \left( \frac{w_{x_m x_{m+1}}}{\max_{k} w_{x_m k}} \right) \cdot \exp\left( -\frac{\Delta t}{\sigma_t} \right)$, where $\sigma_t = 5.0$ years. Across all simple paths of length $\le 3$:
\begin{equation}
    P(u, v) = \max_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{u \to v}^{\le 3}} \prod_{m=0}^{|p|-1} \rho(x_m, x_{m+1}) \in [0, 1]
\end{equation}
If no path exists ($d(u, v) = \infty$), we set $P(u, v) = \epsilon_{\text{inst}} = 0.05$ (institutional cold baseline).

\subsubsection{5. Triadic Closure Penalty $\text{TCP}(u, v)$}
We penalize candidates sharing dense local neighbors within the same community: $\text{TCP}(u, v) = \frac{|N(u) \cap N(v)|}{|N(u) \cup N(v)|} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{\{C(u) = C(v)\}}$. This penalty is zero for all cross-community candidates ($C(u) \neq C(v)$).

\section{Detailed Assessment Methodology}

We formulate a three-tiered evaluation methodology combining quantitative backtesting, a double-blind user study, and usability benchmarking.

\subsection{Phase 1: Historical Temporal Backtest (Quantitative)}

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup and Problem Formulation}
Using historical graph $\mathcal{G}_{\text{train}}$ (2015--2019), we generate ranked top-$K$ candidate partners for every faculty node $u \in V$. Predictions are evaluated against genuine cross-community edges formed during 2020--2024: $E_{\text{ground}} = \{ (u, v) \in E_{\text{eval}} \setminus E_{\text{train}} : C(u) \neq C(v) \}$.

\subsubsection{Baselines and Evaluation Metrics}
We benchmark against four baselines: (1) \textbf{Triadic Closure (Adamic-Adar):} $\text{Score}_{\text{AA}}(u, v) = \sum_{z \in N(u) \cap N(v)} \frac{1}{\log |N(z)|}$; (2) \textbf{Topic-Only (Cosine):} $S_{\text{topic}}(u, v) = \cos(\vec{\tau}_u, \vec{\tau}_v)$; (3) \textbf{Global Betweenness:} $g(v) = \sum_{s \neq v \neq t} \frac{\sigma_{st}(v)}{\sigma_{st}}$; and (4) \textbf{Preferential Attachment:} $k_u \cdot k_v$.

We report Precision@K, Recall@K ($K \in \{5, 10, 20\}$), Mean Average Precision (MAP), Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG@10), and Downstream Citation Impact Ratio: $\text{Impact Ratio} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{Citations}_{\text{cross}}]}{\mathbb{E}[\text{Citations}_{\text{within}}]}$.

\subsection{Phase 2: Randomized Double-Blind User Study}
We are recruiting $N = 15$ UIUC students across CS (4), ECE (3), Bioengineering (3), iSchool (3), and Crop/MatSE (2). Each participant specifies 2--3 courses and instructors they enjoyed. The system generates 15 shuffled, anonymized recommendation cards (5 BridgeFinder, 5 Triadic Closure, 5 Topic-Only). Participants rate cards on 7-point Likert scales across: \emph{Topic Relevance} (1--7), \emph{Novelty/Serendipity} (1--7), and \emph{Outreach Actionability} (1--7). Significance is evaluated via paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction ($\alpha = 0.05$).
